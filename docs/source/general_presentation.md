
# General presentation
NEMESIS-World is a multi-country, multi-sector, recursive dynamic macroeconomic model, inspired by the EU version of the [NEMESIS model](https://www.erasme-team.eu/en/the-nemesis-model/). It is a system of interconnected economic models covering 22 world regions, dedicated to studying issues related to economic development, competitiveness, employment, and long-term structural policies such as climate policy. The primary purpose of the model is to assess the socioeconomic impacts of various policies through scenario-based comparative analyses. The model's core mechanisms rely on the behaviour of representative agents: firms, households, and governments. On the supply side, the model distinguishes 59 different economic activities that produce goods and services using production functions. These activities employ various production factors: capital, energy, low-, medium-, and high-skilled labour, and other intermediate inputs. All sectors are interlinked through inter-industry exchanges matrices and international trade. NEMESIS-World also features a detailed energy-environment module, enabling the model to analyse climate change mitigation policies. This module improves the representation of each region’s energy system by detailing energy use across economic activities and by households. In addition, it allows modelling the power generation sector, by allocating electricity production across different technologies. The model has been developed by SEURECO, a French SME, the data and code to generate the model are open-access and open-source, and publicly available a [Github repository](https://github.com/Seureco/NEMESIS-World).

## 1.1 Regional and sectoral disaggregation

The model offers a global coverage, distinguishing 22 different regions: sixteen individual countries—representing the world’s largest economies—and six aggregated regions, one of which represents the non-disaggregated EU countries. The other aggregated regions cover four different geographical areas, allowing the model to align with the standardised regional classifications used in IPCC reporting. This level of regional detail is crucial for analysing decarbonisation policies, which are often specific to individual countries or regions. In terms of sectoral coverage, the model includes more than fifteen distinct economic activities. This high level of sectoral disaggregation enables detailed assessments of the economic impacts of various structural policies, particularly those targeting specific sectors. The sectors have been selected based on their relevance to greenhouse gas (GHG) mitigation technologies. Accordingly, key industrial sectors such as glass, cement and lime, iron and steel, and aluminium manufacturing are modelled individually. Similarly, the transport sector is disaggregated into sea and coastal shipping, inland waterways, air transport, railways, and other land transport. Beyond technological aspects, the energy supply sector is also comprehensively represented, including sectors such as coal mining, oil and natural gas extraction, and the production of coke, petroleum and nuclear products, gas, and electricity.

## 1.2 Other main features of the model

The model is well-suited for assessing employment impacts of structural changes, due to the detailed representation of economic activities and the disaggregation of workers into three qualification levels: low-, medium-, and high-skilled. This distinction significantly improves the accuracy and policy relevance of the model’s quantitative employment impact assessments, as employment effects are often a central concern for policymakers. Another key feature of the model is its macro-econometric foundation, which distinguishes it from other typical general equilibrium models [Nikas et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-03152-7_1). Specifically, it can be classified as a neo-Keynesian model, allowing for short-term market disequilibria. In particular, the model accounts for non-structural unemployment in the short-run and incorporates behavioural adjustments that gradually converge towards medium- and long-term optimal behaviours. This approach enables the model to capture short-term rigidities while maintaining long-term structural equilibrium. Finally, the model also captures the income flows for the households and the governments, enabling the assessment of the income effects of structural policies.
